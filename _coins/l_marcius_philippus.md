Ancient coins can provide clear messages for their meaning and at other times the story they wish to tell is opaque. Take the obverse of this coin. A noble diademed bust with ANCVS written behind is a clear indication the obverse represents the mythical fourth king of Rome, Ancus Marcius. The reverse dramatically shows an aqueduct with the letters AQVA MAR below clearly spelling out to the viewer this is the Aqua Marcia built by the praetor Q. Marcius Rex between 144-140 BCE [^3]. The moneyer, L. Marcius Philippus, is using this miniature monument to celebrate the Marcii gens through the connection to Rome's king and the aqueduct[^8]. The connection is meaningful as Ancus was reported to be the first to bring water to the city via aqueduct [^1]. The identity and purpose of the equestrian statue is a bit muddled- who is that statue and why is it on top of an aqueduct? 

First, let's disabuse ourselves of the requirement that the reverse depicts a true to life scene. Roman moneyers often invoked mythical imagery, ancient heroes, pulled from various distant family branches, or depicted buildings not yet finished to convey meaning[^7]. Furthermore, a statue on top of an aqueduct is both an engineering impossibility and a political waste[^6]. The aqueduct was buried on its route to the Capitoline and so any standing equestrian statue would be far from the city and the viewing populace with little political value. Consequently, Grueber's claim that *"In commemoration of the great benefit derived from this new water supply the Senate placed an equestrian statue of Marcius on the aqueduct"* [^2] is unsupported in fact and in practice.

So if there wasn't an enormous statue on an aqueduct, who/what was intended for the audience to recall? We have seen Grueber's claim to be it being of Q. Marcius Rex, but Crawford is more circumspect stating clearly "The statue is problematical"[^4] and simply summarizing it as "equestrian". This missing piece of this puzzle is not textual, but archaeological. No remnant of a statue exists on the Capitoline but Mommsen in footnote 4 references [Orelli-Henzen, n° 5088, 6838. - Bullet, de l'lust, arch., 1845, р. 121](https://ia601306.us.archive.org/34/items/bullettinodellin1845inst/bullettinodellin1845inst.pdf) and Crawford (CIL xvi, 5), reference a military diploma from a discharged soldier that states his record of discharge was placed:

> ROMAE • IN • CAPITOLIO • POST • AEDEM • IOVIS • O . M . IN BASI • Q • MARCI • REGIS PR

The partial inscription translates as: *"At Rome, on the Capitoline behind the temple of Jupiter Optimus Maximums on the pedestal [of the statue] of Q. Marcius Rex praetor"*. Stunningly, that find was preserved and is available electronically:

Upon their discharge, legionaries were often granted citizen and a land allotment. This grant of citizenship was publically recorded, so that in theory, their rights could be verified. This diploma from the late Republic was found in Illyria and was likely the soldier's personal copy, with the "official" copy attached to the statue of Q. Marcius Rex, that existed on the Capitoline Hill.

So that's it? The status is Q. Marcius Rex. We have hard archaeological evidence now. Well, new scholarship by Dr. Liv Yarrow in ["The Roman Republic to 49BCE: Using Coins as Sources"](/books/yarrow_roman_republic_to_49_bce) has begun to turn this understanding on its head. Yarrow, building on the work by Hölkeskamp, argues that the statue is of Q. Marcius Tremulus (cos. 306), who was a hero of the early Republic for twice defeating the Samnites and then the Anagni. The key to identifying Tremulus is the depicting of the rider as wearing a toga, rather than military garb. In a brief description of the statue, Pliny specifically calls out this distinction:

>Also there was in front of the temple of the Castors an equestrian statue of Quintus Marcius Tremulus, wearing a **toga** ; he had twice vanquished the Samnites, and by taking Anagni  -[Pliny, Natural History 34:23](https://www.attalus.org/pliny/hn34a.html)

The Temple of Castor and Pollux is at the heart of the Forum and the statue of Tremulus would be visible and recognizable to any Roman citizen. Yarrow argues that the use of Tremulus' statue, paired with the Aqua Marcia, is an attempt to re-appropriate the massive project for the Marcii Philippi (the gens to which our moneyer belongs) and away from the Marcii Reges (the gens of its builder, Q. Marcius Rex). Furthermore, the coinage design itself may have been in contest with similar claims of the Aemili Ledidii over the "ownership" of the aquaduct who earlier issued coins showing an equestrian rider over top an unnamed aquaduct [^10]. Tremelus is also a repeated figure on coins of the Marcii Philippi, all as a rider on horseback.

This long tortured story about the simple question of "who is on the statue?" is why I love late Republican coinage. These small objects are steeped in myth, memory, and politics. For a hobby, it provides the ability to examine beautiful objects, dive into historical books, and weigh arguments of academic titans. It's quite a fun and lengthy journey.
