### 104 BCE - Quaestor in Ostia
Saturninus began his public career in 104 BCE at the worst possible moment. As quaestor at Ostia he was entrusted with Rome's overseas grain supply, meanwhile in Sicily, the praetor [P. Licinius Nerva](/coins/p_licinius_nerva/) sparked the Second Servile War that began to strangle the very grain shipments Saturninus was supposed to manage.[^4] Amid the shortage, the Senate stripped him of the post and handed it to the most senior senator, M. Aemilius Scaurus, the *Princeps Senatus*. Commentators past and present debate whether the snub was intentioal: Cicero called the demotion "an intentional insult."[^5]; Diodorus blames Saturninus's own "laziness" and "debased character" for the removal [^6], while Mike Duncan in The Storm Before Storm doubts whether any capable officer could have handled the situation (@claude: add citation to Mike Duncan, I'll try to find the page number). Regardless of the reasons, the episode establishes a villan "origin story" for subesequent "depravity and mad rage of Lucius Saturninus."[^6a]

### 103 BCE - First Tribunate
Elected to the tribunate the following year, Saturninus began a retribution campaign by establishing Rome's first standing treason court, the *quaestio de maiestate*[^7a], and ensured the jury would be packed with Equestrian supporters. Now, any Senator who stepped out of line could be easily brought to trial on trumped-up charges. The court's first target was the elder Q. Servilius Caepio, held responsible for the catastrophic defeat at Arausio two years before[^8]. The charges were brought by Saturninus' colleage, Norbanus, while two other tribunes attempted to veto the proceedings, but were driven off by violence (an all too often feature of Roman politics now). Conviceted, Capeio was sent into exile. Caepio's counterpart at Arausio, the consul Cn. Mallius Maximus, was likewise driven from Rome that year by a plebiscite sponsored by Saturninus.

Having made enemies, Saturninus now needed friends. Courting the popular general Marius, Satruninus passed a law allocating 100 *iugera* of land in Africa to each of Marius's Jugurthine veterans[^7]. Marius' soldier placated, he now courted the general directly. As the time for consular elections approached, Saturninus engineered a convenient piece of theater in the Forum: Marius attempting to decline the consulship, protesting that he did not want it, while Saturninus, who "had more influence with the people than any other tribune," worked the crowd in his harangues to demand it anyway (@claude: didn't Saturninus acuse Marius of treasure for threatening to leave Rome defenseless). Marius's string of consulships was already unprecedented, but the charade fooled no one; even Plutarch, no enemy of his, says Saturninus was "playing his part at the instigation of Marius, and playing it badly, too."[^10] The scenario worked and Marius was elected to his (NUMBER) consulship.

### 100 BCE - The Final Year
By 100 BC, Saturninus, Marius — now consul for the sixth time — and the praetor C. Servilius Glaucia held Rome's three most powerful offices at once, and the second tribunate opened in violence before it even began: Saturninus's own supporters murdered a rival candidate at the elections themselves.[^12] His grain law forced a heavily subsidized price past his colleagues' resistance; his agrarian law went further still, requiring every senator to swear obedience within five days on pain of exile — a clause built for exactly one target. Numidicus alone refused the oath and chose exile over compliance, "preferring to renounce his country rather than his principles."[^11] The younger Q. Servilius Caepio — almost certainly the son of the elder Caepio just driven into exile — was now quaestor, and met the grain law with violence of his own, physically obstructing its passage in the streets.[^13] When Glaucia's own partisans then murdered a rival consular candidate, the Senate passed the *senatus consultum ultimum* — the same emergency decree Cicero would later cite as settled precedent for crushing the enemies of the state without trial.[^14] Marius, forced to choose between the tribune who had built his career and the Senate's decree, chose the decree. Besieged and surrendered on the Capitol, Saturninus was killed anyway: Marius "did all he could to save the men, but it was of no avail, and when they came down into the forum they were put to death."[^15]

While the historical record is quite clear, whether Saturninus coin's foretold his fate are debatable. The image... 
None of this had happened yet when the coin was struck, but the reverse type reads, in hindsight, like a threat aimed the wrong way. The *harpa* in Saturn's hand is the same blade the god used to castrate his own father, Uranus[^16] — in the hand of a tribune already building a legislative record on the necks of Rome's nobility, it is not hard to read the weapon as pointed at the Senate. Instead, it was Saturninus who was cut down by exactly that kind of state-sanctioned violence, turned on him by the ally who had used him to become consul four times over. Even so, his grain law and his god outlived him. Within the year, the quaestors Piso and the very [Caepio](/coins/capio_piso/) who had fought that law in the street struck a denarius pairing Saturn and the *harpa* with two seated quaestors and the legend AD·FRV·EMV — "for buying grain" — the same god and the same weapon, repurposed from a threat into the machinery of the law it had once opposed.[^17] Metellus Numidicus did not stay in exile long, either: his son spent two years campaigning barefoot and unshaven through the Forum for his father's recall, won it, and took the *cognomen* Pius for the effort — a name he carried onto [his own coinage](/coins/metellus_pius/) a generation later.[^18]

Saturninus's name outlived him by decades, not as a memory to be honored but as a stock example to be invoked. Prosecuting Catiline's fellow conspirators as consul, Cicero pointed to Saturninus's own death as proof that killing citizens without trial carried no political cost at all: Rome's "most illustrious cities not only did not pollute themselves, but even glorified themselves by the blood of Saturninus, and the Gracchi, and Flaccus."[^19] Decades later, rallying the Senate against Antony in the *Philippics*, he reached for the same precedent again, recalling how "all the Scauri, Metelli, Claudii, Catuli, Scaevolas, and Crassi took up arms" against Saturninus and Glaucia and were never called to account for it.[^20] By the time Cicero delivered the *Pro Sestio*, more than forty years after Saturninus's death, his name had settled into the same rhetorical slot as the Gracchi — one of the "Friends of the People" of a bygone generation, whose following, Cicero insisted, needed no bribery to stay loyal.[^21] It is a smaller vindication than his coinage or his grain law, but a durable one: Diodorus had already called him a man of "debased character" within a generation of his death,[^6] and for the rest of the Republic's life, Cicero made sure the label stuck.
