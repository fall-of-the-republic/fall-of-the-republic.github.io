The Wolves of Rome

Created on November 15, 2026

The Capitoline Wolf, Palazzo dei Conservatori, Rome
Source: Author's photo

"The last day is at hand for the Romans...These wolves that made such ravages upon Italian liberty will never vanish until we have cut down the forest that harbours them."

— Velleius Paterculus ii, 27, 21

Few images say “Rome” like the wolf. But the wolf on this denarius of P. Satrienus is not the gentle nurse of Romulus and Remus. She is alone, no twins, no fig tree, no shepherd, and she is on the prowl.

Crawford, the standard catalogue today, calls the head Roma but gives no argument why.2 Grueber, writing for the British Museum sixty years earlier, called it Mars.3 It’s easy to assume the most recent source has settled the debate for all satisfaction. However, when one goes back through the literature you find the older catalogues disagreeing, each for various reasons, and if the latest version simply asserts a position, we are left without an argument for the positon. Working through these reasons yourself exercises the mind and puts any personal conclusion on firmer ground.

Obverse: helmeted head, Mars or Roma?

Reverse: she-wolf walking l.; ROMA above, P SATRIE NVS below

Mars/Roma - Obverse

Grueber’s catalog entry in BMCRR clearly states a young Mars as obverse, but diving into his rich footnotes reveals a deeper conversation. He thinks Mars is more likely because the features look too masculine for a goddess, and notes that Mommsen thought so too4. Babelon looked at the same head and saw long, womanly hair, and seeing the legend ROMA and asserting that ROMA cannot name the wolf, associates Roma as the obverse.5 More recently, Liv Yarrow has come down on Mars too. She argues that an ancient viewer would more naturally have seen Mars, and that he pairs well with the wolf, his sacred animal, on the reverse.6

Resolving this argument is aided by context and deeper reading. Babelon attempts to follow Mommsen, but missed the deeper reading, ROMA names the type, not the design. 7 Reading ROMA as the name of the people who issued the coin has deep roots. Rome’s first silver, the didrachm of the 260s, carries the wolf and twins with ROMANO, “of the Romans,” underneath.8 The first denarii of 211 BCE put ROMA under the galloping Dioscuri for the next century ROMA sits in the exergue below Jupiter, Victory, Luna, and Hercules drawn by centaurs. It is the state’s signature. Then, after about 115 BCE, it fades, and by the Social War it is all but gone.9 By this time the Romans were masters of Italy and no longer needed to say whose coin this was.

Quadrigatus, c. 225–212 BCE (Crawford 28/3): Janiform head of the Dioscuri / Jupiter in a quadriga driven by Victory, ROMA in the exergue
Source: CNG, Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.5)

Anonymous denarius, c. 211 BCE (Crawford 44/5): Roma / the Dioscuri galloping r., ROMA in the exergue
Source: Bibliothèque nationale de France, Gallica (public domain)

That’s what makes the legend on this coin stand out. Satrienus is one of the very last moneyers to use ROMA, which is used not to identify the coin to a new audience, but to re-assert Roman mastery of Italy. Reading the coin as a whole: Mars, the god of war, on one obverse; a fierce wolf on the reverse, a dominant Republic steps back up to the mic and says: “Allow me to re-introduce myself.”

Wolf - Reverse

On to the reverse. The wolf has been on Roman coins from the very start. The first Roman silver coins showed a bold Hercules on the obverse she-wolf suckling Romulus and Remus on the reverse. Later, denarii repeated this scene of a protective and nuturing wolf.

Didrachm, c. 269–266 BCE (Crawford 20/1): Hercules / she-wolf suckling the twins, ROMANO
Source: Numismatica Ars Classica, Auction 150 (2 December 2024), lot 730

Anonymous denarius, c. 115–114 BCE (Crawford 287/1): Roma / Roma seated on shields, the she-wolf suckling the twins at her feet, birds on either side
Source: Bibliothèque nationale de France, Gallica (public domain)

Denarius of Sex. Pompeius, 137 BCE (Crawford 235/1): the shepherd Faustulus finds the twins under the fig tree
Source: CNG, Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.5)

But our wolf here is not nuturing. There are no twins. She is fierce and described by Crawford as the “Capitoline” wolf.10 Many will take that name to mean the Capitoline Wolf in Musei Capitolini today. Even if the bronze is ancient, and radiocarbon dating suggests it may be medieval,11 it has only been on the Capitol since 1471, and nobody knows where it stood before. There have been various wolves around the city throughout history, and all of them, as far as we can tell, were nursing: the Ogulnii’s wolf and twins at the Ruminal fig tree below the Palatine in 296 BCE12, which Crawford thinks the first didrachm copies;8 a bronze wolf by the fig tree in the Comitium, marking a miracle of the augur Attus Navius;13 and a small gilt group of Romulus at the wolf’s teats that Cicero called the nurse of Roman dominion, struck by lightning in 65 BCE.14

The Wolf is ROMA

Denarius of the Italian rebels under C. Papius Mutilus, c. 90 BCE: Bacchus / the Italian bull goring the Roman wolf
Source: © The Trustees of the British Museum, via Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)

A cleaner reading of the whole coin comes from looking at when it was struck. In 91 BCE Rome’s Italian allies, tired of fighting Rome’s wars without the vote, rebelled.15 The rebels struck their own denarii clearly stating their cause. Under the Samnite general Gaius Papius Mutilus, his name in Oscan below, the bull of Italy gores the Roman wolf to the ground and drives its horn into her.16 This characterization of Rome as ravionus wolves was not isolated only to her former allies. Mithridates of Pontus said the expansionist Romans has the “disposition of wolves” and instatiable for blood, tyranny, and riches. 17

In word and in metal, Rome was a wolf, and not a motherly one.
